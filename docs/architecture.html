An auto-research harness for verifiable quantum circuit compilation

A durable experiment substrate, three compute arms priced by what they actually cost, and one scarce reasoning budget spent only where it changes the outcome.

Target  Google Quantum AI — Verifiable Circuit Compilation Benchmark  invsqrt Final deadline  2027-01-20 Drafted  2026-11-11

The property everything else follows from

This competition is unusual: the scoring function runs locally, it is exact, and it is effectively free. Google's kickmix simulator checks a candidate circuit against millions of classical inputs at roughly 6×1010 instructions per second. You never need Kaggle to know your score — the five submissions per day are leaderboard calibration, not feedback.

That inverts the usual economics of an agentic research loop. Normally the model must be right, because nothing else can tell you whether it was. Here a perfect oracle sits downstream of every guess, so correctness is cheap to establish and volume becomes the scarce input rather than judgment.

A weak model with an exact verifier beats a strong model without one, provided you can afford enough attempts. Your DGX Spark can afford a great many.

Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B activates roughly 3B parameters per token. It will not invent a novel reversible-arithmetic construction. But it does not need to be right — it needs to be right occasionally, because the verifier identifies which attempts those were in seconds and the rest cost nothing to discard. Meanwhile the genuinely scarce resource, Opus reasoning on a $20 plan, gets spent on the one thing volume cannot substitute for: proposing a different construction.

So the design question is not "how do I build an agent loop." It is how do I route each kind of work to the cheapest resource that can do it, and keep the results when the loop itself is inevitably rewritten.

Four arms, priced by what they cost

The arms are separated by the scarcity of the resource they consume, not by what they do. That is the only split that matters when one input is metered in dollars and the others are free.

Construct

scarce Opus
~8×/day
~100k tok each

Authors an entirely new construction family — a different approximation scheme, a cheaper reversible primitive, a new table/refinement split. Receives a hypothesis and the current Pareto front; returns a working program. This is the only arm that can produce a step change, and the main consumer of your token budget.

Mutate

free Qwen3-Next-80B
DGX Spark
continuous

Best-of-N within an existing family. Given the top-k programs plus diffs of mutations that previously worked, it proposes variants. A 5% hit rate is fine because the oracle filters for free. This is the grind, and it runs 24/7 at zero marginal cost.

Tune

compute plain code
16 cores
continuous

No model involved. Simulated annealing, beam search, peephole rewriting and numerical optimisation over a family's exposed parameters. Worth perhaps the last 20–30% of a family's potential — real, but never the step change. Keeps the CPUs busy whenever the verification queue is short.

Steer

scarce Opus or Sonnet
~6×/day
~30k tok each

Reads a prepared digest — current front, recent decisions, the hypothesis board, what failed and why, budget state — and returns structured JSON: which families to kill, what to try next, how to split effort. Single-shot, no tool access. That restriction is the entire budget control; an agentic session costs 20× a consult.

Where I was wrong in the first pass

I originally framed the classical arm as carrying 95% of the value. It does not. Tuning a fixed construction's knobs cannot find a better construction, and the published precedent Google cites — agents halving Toffoli counts on ecdsa.fail — came from agents writing new constructions. Tune is the exploitation arm; Construct and Mutate are where the score moves.

The substrate

You asked for a layer that does not change, so a rewritten loop can pick up where the last one stopped. That makes this the most important part of the design, and it argues for event sourcing: an append-only log of what was learned, with everything else derived from it.

substrate/                   # STABLE. Changes only by explicit migration.
  ledger/events-*.jsonl      # append-only, one JSON event per line
  blobs/sha256/ab/cd/<hash>   # circuits, program source, logs
  schema/event.v1.json
  index.sqlite               # DERIVED — delete and replay to rebuild
  cli.py                     # the ONLY API: append / query / rebuild / gc

programs/invsqrt/           # evolving construction families
loops/v1_funsearch/         # DISPOSABLE orchestrator
briefs/                     # cached prompt prefixes for the PI
inbox/ outbox/              # async steering channel
dashboard/

The invariant that makes loops swappable: a loop may only reach the substrate through cli.py. Nothing else writes to the ledger, ever. Write loop v2 in a different language with a different harness; it replays the log and continues.

Blobs are regenerable — store recipes, garbage-collect outputs

A serialised candidate circuit is large; the program plus its config that produced it is tiny and deterministic. So the ledger keeps the recipe forever and the GC keeps only Pareto-optimal circuits, recent ones, and direct lineage ancestors. Without this policy blob storage grows past 100 GB; with it, a few GB. See the appendix.

The verification ladder

The oracle is cheap but not instant, and it becomes the binding constraint once Mutate is running at volume. So verification is tiered, and almost everything dies at rung one.

Never trust a single seed

The verifier seeds its tests from your circuit's SHA-256 combined with a secret salt, and any edit to a circuit draws a fresh test set. A candidate that passes on one seed may be lucky, not correct. Every candidate therefore carries a seed-robustness count, and nothing gets submitted on fewer than four independent seeds.

Steering, without being in the loop

You want as few interactive components as possible, so the human is not a step in the pipeline. Instead there is one Admin agent — a persistent chat surface backed by the substrate.

  • It reads the current front, running jobs, budget state, open blockers.
  • It writes your asks into the ledger as real events: priority overrides, kill orders, new hypotheses, "pause Mutate", "go read this paper".
  • It surfaces what the PI could not resolve, stalls, and budget warnings — next time you happen to look.

The critical property: the loop never blocks on you. When the PI hits something it cannot settle, it writes to inbox/ and drops to lower-value work. Your steering lands in the same ledger as the PI's decisions, so it is equally durable and equally auditable. Model routing is cheap-first: Qwen answers status questions for free, and only genuine reasoning requests escalate to Sonnet or Opus.

Budget governor

A $20 Claude plan is roughly 17.4M tokens per week (third-party measurement — confirm with /usage), on rolling five-hour windows. The failure mode you named — burning a week in an hour — comes from one specific mistake: letting the model hold tools.

 Separate buckets per class, not one global counter — otherwise a burst of program-authoring silently starves strategic steering.

Three rules carry the whole thing. Single-shot only for automated calls: an agentic session that reads files costs 200–800k tokens, a digest-driven consult costs 30k. Hard reserve for interactive use, so the loop can never lock you out of your own tooling. Graceful degradation — when Construct is exhausted the loop keeps grinding on Mutate and Tune rather than halting; it loses new ideas, not progress.

Prompt caching does real work here too. The standing brief — competition spec, the invsqrt contract, house rules — is a stable prefix, and consults land inside the one-hour cache TTL, so the fixed context is roughly a tenth of list price after the first call.

Harness choices

Your orchestrator should not be an agent harness. It is a long-running scheduler with a budget ledger and a database; agent harnesses are built for interactive sessions, and coupling the durable layer to one would defeat the swappability you asked for. Target: invsqrt

Easiest of the three by a clear margin, which is what you want while the harness itself is unproven.

  • One artifact. A single .kmb/.kmx file — no .npz factorization, no multi-circuit folder, no starter kit to integrate.
  • Simplest cost function. Q × (Toffolis + T/4): one scalar, both terms linear. By contrast dqi weights qubits quadratically and adds a failure-rate term; dfthcsos carries four hard rejection constraints.
  • Deep classical literature. Fixed-point 1/√x has decades of prior art — Newton–Raphson, piecewise polynomial, lookup plus refinement, bit-trick seeds. Real knowledge for Construct to deploy.
  • Memory-light. A 22-qubit input register and ~260 qubits total. kickmix simulates classical reversible logic, so state is bits per shot, not amplitudes.
  • Baseline to beat: Q = 260, 7,735 Toffolis.

One consequence to internalise now. The metric is a geometric mean over all three benchmarks, so with the other two left at baseline, overall score = (invsqrt improvement)1/3.

 Don't be startled when a hard-won 3× shows as 1.44. The machinery then clones onto a second benchmark for a second factor.

Honest ceiling

2,849 teams, $150,000 in prizes, and the strong entrants will be quantum-computing specialists running agents with real domain priors. A solo loop on a $20 plan plus one 3B-active local model is not the favourite. What this setup can plausibly do is beat the baseline substantially on one benchmark and leave you owning a harness that transfers. Aim there; treat the leaderboard as a thermometer.

Build order

No models until step four. Most auto-research loops fail because nobody established that the evaluator was trustworthy first.

  1. Make the oracle trustworthy

    flake.nix with an FHS-wrapped Python so Kaggle's prebuilt kickmix.so loads on NixOS; authenticate Kaggle; reproduce the reference invsqrt circuit and confirm it verifies at cost exactly 260 × 7,735. Nothing else matters until this is green.

  2. Substrate

    Events, blobs, cli.py, rebuildable SQLite index, GC policy. Ingest the baseline as candidate zero.

  3. Tune arm and dashboard v0

    Classical search over the reference construction's parameters, plus the verification ladder. You get a real progress picture before any model is involved.

  4. Mutate arm

    Qwen on the Spark behind an OpenAI-compatible endpoint, best-of-N, oracle-filtered. First point at which the loop is autonomous.

  5. Construct and Steer

    Both behind Advisor, with per-bucket budget accounting and the standing brief as a cached prefix.

  6. Admin agent

    The async steering channel, once there is something worth steering.

Open questions

  • Does Opus exist on your plan? Sources conflict on whether the $20 tier includes it. Check /usage before the budget table above is worth trusting.
  • Subscription or API for Construct? I'd default to claude -p on your subscription and flip to metered API credits only if your interactive quota gets squeezed, or you want to run harder than a consumer plan is intended for. One driver swap either way.
  • Is Qwen's hit rate high enough? The volume argument assumes something like 5% of mutations are valid improvements. If it turns out to be 0.1%, Mutate is theatre and the budget should shift to Construct. This is measurable in week one and should be measured before scaling.
  • Does one 22-bit construction family dominate? If the entire field converges on the same approximation scheme, the differentiator is Tune, not Construct — which would invert the arm priorities.

Cost of one verification

A full invsqrt verification is 3,133,452 × 64 ≈ 2.0 × 108 shots. At 105 instructions per circuit that is 2 × 1013 instructions:

2.0×108 shots × 105 instr = 2.0×1013 instr 2.0×1013 ÷ 6.0×1010 instr/s = 334 core-seconds per seed
on 16 cores, 1 seed → ~21 s wall on 16 cores, 4 seeds → ~84 s wall (1,336 core-s)

Construction is the surprise. At 2×107 instr/s it is three thousand times slower per instruction than simulation — but a single invsqrt circuit is only 105 instructions, so building one costs ~5 ms. That flips the bottleneck at the cheap rungs: a smoke test's kickmix work is ~7 ms, so Python interpreter and process startup dominate it entirely. Use a warm persistent worker pool, never a fresh process per candidate — that one decision is worth more than an order of magnitude at the smoke rung.

Throughput budget — and the actual bottleneck

16 cores × 86,400 s = 1.38M core-seconds per day. Reserving ~20% for the OS, dashboard, SQLite and the Spark client leaves ~1.1M usable core-seconds/day. Pipeline a day's candidates through the ladder, assuming 20% survive smoke and 5% of all candidates are front-improving enough to deserve full verification:

per candidate: 1.0 × 0.2 (smoke) + 0.2 × 0.3 (screen) + 0.05 × 1336 (full) = 0.2 + 0.06 + 66.8 = ~67 core-seconds
CPU ceiling: 1.1M ÷ 67 = ~16,000 candidates/day Qwen ceiling: ~10,000 candidates/day (see GPU section)
without the ladder (full-verify everything): 1.1M ÷ 1336 = ~820/day
The ladder is what converts CPU from the binding constraint into slack. With it, generation on the Spark is the bottleneck — without it, CPU is, by a factor of twelve.

The useful consequence: verification consumes only about 12% of available CPU at Qwen's realistic output rate. The remaining ~960k core-seconds/day — roughly eleven core-days per calendar day — is free capacity for the Tune arm, which should be scheduled at low priority and simply absorb whatever the verification queue is not using.
